The TAB and offshore bookmakers — how betting works in NZ

New Zealand's betting landscape is simpler than its casino one. The TAB, operated by TAB NZ, is the only licensed domestic bookmaker, and profits are returned to New Zealand racing and sporting codes. It's the default for many Kiwis and covers racing and sport well.

It's also not illegal for New Zealanders to bet with offshore bookmakers, and many international books accept Kiwi accounts. Players use them for wider markets, different odds, or features the TAB doesn't offer. The choice usually comes down to whether you value backing local racing through the TAB or want the extra markets and prices an offshore book can carry.

The odds-margin question — an honest comparison

Most NZ betting guides dismiss the TAB out of hand and steer you straight to offshore books. We don't, because the reality is more nuanced. The TAB is not automatically the worst-priced option, and an offshore book is not automatically the best — it depends entirely on the market.

The number that matters is the margin (or overround): the edge a bookmaker builds into a market above the true chance of each outcome. On a two-way market where both sides are genuinely 50/50, fair odds would be 2.00 / 2.00. If a book prices it 1.90 / 1.90, it has baked in roughly a 5.3% margin; at 1.95 / 1.95 the margin is closer to 2.6% and much better for you. Multiply that gap across hundreds of bets a season and it becomes real money.

Where the TAB tends to carry a wider margin is multi-runner racing fields and niche or lower-liquidity sport markets — the overround on a full 14-horse field can run high, and some offshore books shade those prices tighter. Where the TAB is genuinely competitive is the marquee stuff Kiwis actually bet in volume: All Blacks tests, big Super Rugby and NRL fixtures, and Black Caps internationals often sit within a few cents of the offshore price, and occasionally the TAB is the best price in the market. It also settles cleanly in NZD with no conversion, and its racing tote pools are the deepest in the country.

The takeaway isn't "TAB good" or "offshore good" — it's that no single account is best on everything. The sharpest punters hold two or three and back the best price on each market, every time.

Is online sports betting legal in New Zealand?

Yes — with an important structural detail most guides skip. Betting on sport and racing is legal for New Zealanders, and the domestic market is built around a single licensed operator.

gavelTAB NZ's July 2025 exclusivity

The TAB, run by TAB NZ, is the only bookmaker licensed to operate inside New Zealand. In July 2025 that position was reinforced: TAB NZ entered a long-term strategic partnership with global operator Entain, which took over the commercial running of the TAB and secured exclusive rights to domestic betting. The Betcha brand operates alongside the TAB within that same licensed framework, so when you see Betcha you are effectively inside the TAB NZ / Entain arrangement rather than at an independent local competitor. In exchange for exclusivity, the arrangement returns guaranteed funding to New Zealand racing and sporting codes.

That exclusivity applies to operating within New Zealand. It does not make it illegal for a New Zealander to hold an account with an offshore bookmaker. Offshore books licensed in jurisdictions such as Malta (MGA) or Curaçao are not licensed here, and they cannot legally advertise into New Zealand, but Kiwis do use them — for wider markets, different prices, or features the TAB doesn't carry. The law targets who may operate domestically, not the individual punter.

None of this is changed by the Online Casino Gambling Act 2026, which passed in May 2026 and covers online casino games — pokies and table games — under a new Department of Internal Affairs licensing regime. Sports and race betting sits outside that Act and stays under the TAB NZ framework. New Zealand's betting calendar has its own rhythm. These are the codes that draw the most turnover, and what to look for in a book that covers them well.

The big one. All Blacks tests, Super Rugby Pacific, the NPC and internationals. Look for deep prop markets — first try-scorer, winning margin, half-time/full-time.

NRL (rugby league)

The Warriors carry huge Kiwi interest through the NRL season. Anytime try-scorer, line and total-points markets are the staples.

Cricket

Football

The Premier League leads for Kiwi punters, alongside the A-League and major internationals. Multis and both-teams-to-score are perennial favourites.

Basketball & more

The NBA, the Breakers in the NBL, plus tennis, golf and UFC round out a busy Kiwi betting week across the calendar.

A book worth using covers the domestic competitions, not just the marquee internationals. If it lists the NPC and the A-League as deeply as the All Blacks, it's built for New Zealand.

Rugby union — All Blacks, Super Rugby and the NPC

Rugby is the anchor of the New Zealand betting year. All Blacks tests draw the biggest turnover, but Kiwi punters also bet Super Rugby Pacific week to week and follow the NPC provincial competition closely. A good book prices more than the head-to-head: winning margin bands, first and anytime try-scorer, half-time/full-time doubles, total points over/under, and a bet builder for same-game multis. On All Blacks fixtures the market is deep and prices are sharp, so this is exactly where lining up two or three books pays off.

Rugby league — the NRL and the Warriors

The NZ Warriors carry enormous Kiwi interest across the NRL season, and league betting turnover spikes on every Warriors game and through the finals. The staple markets are the line (handicap), total points, and anytime try-scorer, with first try-scorer and player-tries props at the deeper books. State of Origin is a mid-season betting event in its own right.

Cricket — the Black Caps and T20 franchises

Cricket runs almost year-round for New Zealand punters. Black Caps tests, ODIs and T20 internationals anchor the local calendar, and franchise T20 leagues around the world keep in-play cricket ticking over in the off-season. Cricket is the code where a fast, responsive live product matters most — odds shift on every ball, and a laggy in-play feed will cost you. Look for top batter, method-of-dismissal, over/under runs and session markets alongside the match result.

Football — Premier League, A-League and internationals

Football is the most-bet global code among Kiwis. The English Premier League leads, with the A-League, the FIFA World Cup and Champions League nights drawing crowds. Both-teams-to-score, over/under 2.5 goals, correct score and multis are the perennial favourites, and a bet builder lets you stack a scorer, the result and cards into one price.

Racing — thoroughbred, harness and greyhound

Racing is where the TAB is strongest and where the tote-versus-fixed-odds choice matters most. New Zealand runs three racing codes, and a book built for Kiwis covers all three:

  • Thoroughbred (gallops): the biggest racing pool, from midweek country meetings to the spring carnival and the Melbourne Cup. Win, place, each-way, exotics (trifecta, first four) and fixed odds are all offered.
  • Harness (trotting): a strong New Zealand tradition, with major nights like the New Zealand Trotting Cup at Addington drawing serious turnover. The TAB carries the deepest harness pools.
  • Greyhound racing: fast, frequent fields that suit quick-turnaround betting. Note that greyhound racing in New Zealand is being wound down under a government phase-out, so its long-term availability is changing — check current fixtures before you plan around it.

On racing, decide up front whether you want the tote (pari-mutuel — your dividend depends on the final pool and isn't locked until the off) or fixed odds (your price is locked when you bet). The TAB's tote pools are the deepest in the country; offshore books are fixed-odds only. For big fields, compare the fixed price against the likely tote dividend before you commit.

Bet types explained for Kiwi punters

Whether you're new to betting or just want a refresher, these are the bet types you'll meet at every New Zealand sportsbook.

Live and in-play betting

Live betting — placing bets while the match is underway, with odds that shift on every play — is now the busiest part of most sportsbooks. It suits New Zealand's viewing habits, where a lot of sport is watched late at night or early morning, and a good live product keeps you in the game.

  • Odds refresh speed: the best books update in-play prices in near real time. Laggy odds mean you're betting on a moment that's already passed.
  • Cash out: lets you settle a bet early for a guaranteed return before the final whistle. Handy for locking in a profit or cutting a loss on a multi.
  • Live streaming: some books stream fixtures directly, so you can watch and bet in one place. Availability varies by sport and rights.
  • Bet builders: combine multiple markets from one match — a try-scorer, the winner and total points — into a single priced bet.

Test the in-play product before you rely on it: place a small live bet and see how quickly it settles. A book that's slow to settle a $5 in-play bet will frustrate you on a big one.

Odds and margins explained

Odds are the price of a bet, and over a season the price is what decides whether you're ahead. New Zealand books quote in decimal odds, where the number is your total return per dollar staked — $10 at 2.50 returns $25 including your stake.

The figure to compare across books is the margin (or overround) — how much the bookmaker builds into a market above true probability. A two-way market priced at 1.90 / 1.90 carries about a 5% margin; the same market at 1.95 / 1.95 is sharper and better for you. Line up the same fixture across books and back the best price.

How to find value

Value isn't about backing favourites or longshots — it's about backing a price that's bigger than the true chance warrants. In practice, for a Kiwi punter, that means three habits: hold more than one account so you can always take the best available price; focus on markets you actually understand, where you can judge whether a price is generous; and track your bets so you know whether your judgement is beating the margin over time. Chasing the biggest odds on a market you don't follow is not value — it's guessing.

Sign-up offers and welcome bonuses

Read a betting welcome offer the way you'd read a casino bonus — the headline is marketing, the conditions are the deal. A "bet $10 get $50" promo almost always means bonus bets, not cash: you keep the winnings, not the stake, and there's usually a turnover requirement and a short expiry attached.

Two things to know for New Zealand specifically. First, the licensed TAB is limited in the inducements it can offer under NZ rules, so its promos are more modest than the offshore headlines — which are larger precisely because they're pitched from jurisdictions with looser advertising rules. Second, a big one-off offer is worth far less than sharp everyday odds. The account you'll actually profit from over a season is the one with the tightest margins, not the one that gave you a free bet on day one.

  • Work out the real value: a "bet $10 get $50 in bonus bets" is not $50 of value — bonus bets typically return only the winnings, so their cash-equivalent worth is a fraction of the face figure.
  • Check the turnover: how many times must you wager before withdrawing, and at what minimum odds?
  • Check the expiry: a bonus bet you have to use in seven days on a quiet sporting week can be worthless.
  • Never open an account for the offer alone: pick the book on markets and price, and treat the offer as a small bonus on top.

Common mistakes New Zealand bettors make

Most losing habits aren't about picking the wrong team — they're about process. These are the ones we see most often.

Chasing the biggest offer

Opening an account for a headline "bet $10 get $50" and ignoring the everyday odds. The margin you pay all season dwarfs a one-off bonus bet.

Betting on one site only

Sticking to a single book means you take whatever price it offers. Holding two or three lets you back the best available price on every market.

Skipping verification

Leaving KYC until a winning bet lands is the number-one cause of a delayed payout. Verify when you open the account.

Chasing losses in-play

Live betting makes it easy to fire in a bigger bet to win back a loss. That's how a bad night becomes a worse one — set a limit and stick to it.

Get the process right — shop for price, verify early, stake within a set budget, and treat betting as entertainment — and you'll get more enjoyment for your money regardless of how the results fall.
